# Shallow cable-tool oil economics, US 1885–1905 (Beaumont focus)

Research notes behind docs/DESIGN.md §8, "Historical economics" (November 2026).

**Labels.** DOCUMENTED means a source states the figure, seen in that source's text as quoted by a search engine. UNVERIFIED means a figure recalled from secondary knowledge but not confirmed in a source during this research: check it before relying on it. ESTIMATE means a derivation, with the method shown.

**Research limits.** Direct page fetches from the research environment were blocked for almost every host, including tshaonline.org, pubs.usgs.gov, nber.org, fred.stlouisfed.org, eia.gov, gutenberg.org, archive mirrors and library.wisc.edu. Only Wikipedia could be opened. Every DOCUMENTED figure below therefore comes from search-engine summaries of the cited pages. Three primary volumes would close most gaps: USGS *Mineral Resources* 1890–1902 (petroleum chapters), Hayes & Kennedy, USGS Bulletin 212 (1903), and the NBER chapter by Williamson, Andreano & Menezes. Allowing those hosts would let them be read.

## 8. Contract drilling income

The documented contractor rates are $1.00, then $0.60, then $0.45 per ft for 1877–98 [1], and $0.90–$1.00 per ft around 1900–1910 [2]. The Hamill brothers drilled the Lucas well under contract to 1,200 ft [7], but I found no rate for it. The 1893 Spindletop hole was drilled by Walter B. Sharp, a Dallas water-well contractor [6]. In 1894 Corsicana contracted the American Well & Prospecting Co. to drill three city water wells [14]. ESTIMATE: in 1890, a contractor's gross income was about $450–$600 per 1,000 ft, with roughly 30–60 drilling days on shallow Gulf Coast holes.
